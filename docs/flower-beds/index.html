Flower Beds: a free Queens-style puzzle, every day

Plant one flower in every row, every column and every bed – and never let two flowers touch, not even at the corners. Three new boards grow every day at midnight UTC, each with exactly one solution you can reach without guessing.

How to play Flower Beds

The board is a square garden divided into beds. Each bed has its own soft color, its own pattern and its own name – Rose, Iris, Fern, Mint and so on – so you can tell the beds apart even if you can’t see the colors. A board with six rows also has six columns and six beds, and you plant exactly six flowers.

Three rules decide where they go:

  1. One flower in every row and every column.
  2. One flower in every bed.
  3. Flowers never touch – not side by side and not diagonally. Around every flower, all eight neighboring squares stay empty.

Tap a square once to mark it empty (✕), tap again to plant a flower, and a third time to clear it. You can drag across several squares to mark them all empty in one stroke. On a keyboard, move with the arrow keys and press F for a flower, X for empty and Backspace to clear. With “Show blocked squares” switched on, every square that a planted flower rules out shows a faint dot, so you never have to cross off the obvious ones yourself. A flower that clashes with another one gets a red outline, but nothing ever tells you whether a flower is right before you finish – that part is yours.

If you get stuck, press Why?. Instead of revealing a square, the hint names the next deduction in one sentence and outlines the squares it is about, for example: “Row 4 has only one open square left, C4, so its flower goes there.” You decide whether to apply it.

What the three levels ask of you

The levels are not just bigger boards. Each puzzle is solved by our own solver, which uses a fixed list of techniques ordered from simple to advanced, and a puzzle is published at a level only if it needs that level’s technique – and nothing harder.

LevelBoardTechniques you needLogical steps (average)

Grove8 × 8, 8 beds+ several beds squeezed into the same number of rows or columns20.0

A “logical step” is one deduction the solver writes down, such as “these three squares are empty”. Ruling out squares around a planted flower is not counted, because the board does that for you. In all 60 Grove puzzles of the test period the multi-bed technique was needed at least once; in 45 of the 60 Bloom puzzles the solver also needed the “would block a whole bed” idea.

If you are new to this kind of puzzle, start at Sprout for a few days. The two ideas behind Bloom and Grove are explained step by step in Star Battle strategy: 7 techniques and in the “three beds, three rows” trick.

A small example, solved

Good first moves are almost always in the smallest beds and in the corners. A bed of two squares side by side, for instance, leaves its flower in one of two squares – and whichever it is, the squares directly above and below that pair are touched by it, so they can be marked empty at once. A bed that lies entirely inside one row claims that row’s flower, and every other square of the row can be crossed off.

Once a flower is planted, the board fills in its consequences: its row, its column, its bed and its eight neighbors are all blocked. Then look for a row, column or bed with only one open square left. On Sprout boards, that single idea – the last open square – is enough to solve the whole puzzle.

How the “Why?” hints are made

The hint is not a lookup in the answer key. When you press Why?, the page first checks your marks against the unique solution. If a flower or an empty mark is wrong, the hint points at it and says so – it does not reveal anything else. If everything is right, the same solver that graded the puzzle looks at your current board and reports the easiest deduction it can find, in the order Sprout → Bloom → Grove. That way a hint never jumps ahead with an advanced trick when a simple one is available.

Each hint is a single sentence and comes with highlighted squares: a purple outline for the squares the reasoning is about and a dashed box for the squares it decides. “Apply this step” writes the deduction onto the board. Hints are counted in your result, but they never cost you anything in your garden.

Beds you can tell apart without color

Many region puzzles rely on color alone. About one in twelve men and one in two hundred women have some form of red-green color vision deficiency, so a board where the only difference between two regions is a shade of green and a shade of red is unfair to a lot of players. On Daily Logic Garden every bed has three distinguishing features: a muted tone, a texture (dots, stripes in two directions, cross-hatch, rings and so on) and a thick border wherever two beds meet. The legend under the board names every bed, and screen readers announce each square with its coordinates and bed name, such as “C4, Iris bed, open”.

The board also works in dark mode, with large touch targets on phones and complete keyboard control on a computer. If you prefer paper, the Print button prints today’s board in black and white with the patterns and the first two letters of each bed’s name in its top-left square.

Where this kind of puzzle comes from

Flower Beds belongs to a family of placement puzzles that puzzle books usually call Star Battle: place a fixed number of stars in every row, column and region so that no two stars touch. The version with one star per region became widely known through a daily puzzle on a professional networking site, which is why many people search for it as a “Queens” puzzle; we use the term only to describe the genre. Flower Beds is our own implementation with our own boards, generated fresh from the date – nothing is copied from any other publisher.

Unlike chess queens, the flowers here do not attack along diagonals across the board; they only block their eight immediate neighbors. That is exactly the one-star Star Battle rule, and it is why the puzzle can be solved by pure counting and elimination.

Questions and answers

Is there always exactly one solution?

Yes. Every board is checked twice before you see it: our logic solver must finish it without guessing, and a separate brute-force search must find exactly one way to plant the flowers. Boards that fail either check are thrown away.

When does a new puzzle appear?

At midnight UTC – 7 p.m. in New York during standard time, 4 p.m. in Los Angeles. The page uses your device clock converted to UTC, and everyone in the world gets the same three boards.

Can I play earlier days?

Yes. The archive lists the last 30 days with links to all three levels. Those boards are recreated from the date on your device, so they are identical to what everyone played that day.

Do hints or a slow time hurt my garden?

No. A solved puzzle always plants its flower in your garden, no matter how many hints you used or how long it took. There are no streaks to lose.

Why can two flowers not touch diagonally?

That is the rule that makes the puzzle work. Without it, one-per-row, one-per-column and one-per-bed alone would allow many more arrangements, and the boards could not be built to have a single solution with so few beds.
