Garden Path: draw one line through every square

Start on stone 1, end on the last stone, visit the numbered stones in order and walk through every square of the garden exactly once. It sounds like a doodle; it plays like a proper logic puzzle.

How to play Garden Path

The garden is a grid of squares with a few numbered stepping stones. Draw a single path that:

  1. starts on stone 1 and ends on the stone with the highest number,
  2. passes the other stones in number order – 2 before 3, 3 before 4,
  3. moves only up, down, left or right, never diagonally,
  4. visits every square exactly once, and
  5. never crosses a hedge – the thick dark bars that appear on Bloom and Grove boards.

Put your finger (or mouse) on stone 1 and drag. The path follows you square by square. Drag back over the last square to undo a step, or tap any square on your path to cut it back to that point. You can also tap the next square instead of dragging. On a keyboard, focus the board and use the arrow keys; Backspace steps back.

When you ask for a hint, the answer may be about a part of the garden your path has not reached yet – for example “C5 has only two ways left to go, so the path must pass B5 → C5 → D5.” Applied hints are drawn as a dotted purple guide line, and steps that can never be used are marked with a small red bar. When your path reaches a dotted guide, it follows it automatically.

A solved example

The best place to start is almost never stone 1. Look at the corners first: a corner square has only two neighbors, so if it is not an end of the path, the path must enter through one and leave through the other. That fixes two steps at once. The same is true for any square that has only two usable neighbors left – because the rest are hedges, or because they are already full.

A square that already has two path segments is “full”: no further step may enter it. Full squares around a corner often create a new two-exit square next to them, and so the path grows from the edges inwards, long before it is connected to stone 1.

The Grove idea is the subtle one. Often you know two separate pieces of the path, each passing some stones. If joining them would put the stones out of order – 3, then 6, then 4 – that connection can never be used. On all 60 Grove boards in the test period, the solver needed this argument at least once; on Sprout and Bloom boards it is never required.

Why every square, and why the stones

A path that visits every square of a grid exactly once is what mathematicians call a Hamiltonian path. On an empty grid there are a great many of them, so on its own the task would be a doodle with countless answers. The numbered stones and hedges cut those possibilities down until exactly one path is left – and our generator keeps removing stones as long as the puzzle stays uniquely solvable at its level. The stones you see are the minimum our process found for that board, not decoration.

Counting tells you a lot as well. Color the grid like a chessboard: every step moves from a light square to a dark one or back. On a 5 × 5 board with 25 squares, a path through all of them must start and end on the color that has 13 squares. That is a nice check for your own designs, although the daily boards never require it.

Tips for the drawing itself

  • Draw slowly near hedges. The path can’t cross a hedge; if a drag seems to stop, there is a hedge between the two squares.
  • Use the head marker. The yellow dot shows the current end of your path. Keyboard arrows always move from there.
  • Back up without fear. Undo, dragging back and tapping an earlier square all shorten the path; nothing else on the board changes.
  • Ask for a hint to plan ahead. The dotted guide lines from hints stay on the board even when your path is somewhere else, which turns the puzzle into a planning game if you want it to be.

When the path covers every square and ends on the last stone, the board is solved and a fern grows in your garden.

Questions and answers

Can the path cross itself?

No. Every square is visited exactly once, so the path can never cross or touch a square it has already used.

Do I have to start drawing at stone 1?

Your drawn path always starts at stone 1, but you can plan anywhere: hints and your own deductions about distant corners are shown as dotted guide lines, and your path follows them when it gets there.

What are the thick bars?

Hedges. The path may not step between two squares separated by a hedge. They appear on Bloom and Grove boards and replace some of the stones that would otherwise be needed.

Is there a time limit?

No. The timer only appears in your result line after you finish, and it pauses while the tab is in the background.

Is every board solvable without trial and error?

Yes. Our solver must complete each board with the techniques of its level before it is published, and a separate search confirms that only one path exists.
