Binary puzzle rules: no three in a row, equal counts

Binary puzzles – Takuzu, binairo, our Sun & Shade – rest on two rules. This guide explains both, shows the three basic deductions, and then walks through the counting techniques that harder boards need, using real rows from our puzzles.

The two rules

  1. No three in a row. Never three equal symbols next to each other horizontally or vertically.
  2. Equal counts. Each row and each column holds the same number of each symbol – half and half.

Some publishers add a third rule: no two rows (or columns) may be identical. Our Sun & Shade boards don’t use it; every board is unique under the two rules alone. If you play elsewhere, check which rule set applies, because the third rule allows some extra deductions.

Pair, sandwich, count

These three moves are mechanical once you know them: scan every row and column for two equal neighbors, for equal symbols with one gap, and for lines that already have half of one kind. On our 6 × 6 Sprout boards they are all you need.

How many valid lines are there?

Counting valid lines tells you why the puzzle gets harder with size. We counted every line of a given length that has equal counts and no three in a row:

A row of six has only 14 possible patterns, so a couple of known squares narrow it down quickly. A row of ten has 84, and you often have to reason about the whole line rather than about neighbors.

The “last one” technique

Our hint at that moment: “Row 1 still needs 1 more sun and 3 more shades; every way to finish it without three in a row puts sun on E1 and shade on D1, F1 and G1.”

When a line is missing just one symbol of one kind, that single symbol can only go where placing the other kind everywhere else does not create three in a row. Try each open square as the position of the lone symbol: usually only one works. This is the Bloom technique on our site; every Bloom board in our test period used it at least once.

Full-line counting

On 10 × 10 Grove boards you meet lines missing two or more of each kind. The idea is the same, with more cases: list the ways to finish the line that obey both rules, and look for squares that are the same in all of them. With practice you don’t write the list – you notice, for example, that two remaining suns cannot both fit into the left gap without three shades forming on the right.

Our solver performs this check line by line and reports the squares that every valid completion shares. It never needs to combine guesses across several lines on the published boards.

Good habits

  • After each new symbol, re-check its row and its column for pairs and sandwiches – most moves cascade.
  • Keep a running count per line; “three suns already” on a 6-wide row is the fastest deduction there is.
  • When stuck, choose the line with the fewest open squares and enumerate.

Rows and columns together

Every square belongs to one row and one column, and a deduction in one direction is often the key for the other. A common rhythm is: finish what the pair and sandwich moves give in the rows, then switch to the columns and do the same, then come back. Each pass usually opens a few new squares. When neither direction gives anything, choose the line – row or column – with the fewest open squares and apply the last-one or full-line count to it.

It also helps to know which mistakes are easy to make. The most frequent one is miscounting a line of eight or ten: four suns look like three when two of them sit at the far ends. Count in pairs, left to right, and keep the half-way number in mind – three for six squares, four for eight, five for ten. Our board outlines a line in red as soon as it has more of one symbol than allowed, which catches most slips early.

Which technique each level needs

On Daily Logic Garden, Sprout boards (6 × 6) are solved with pair, sandwich and count alone. Bloom boards (8 × 8) need the last-one technique at least once. Grove boards (10 × 10) need full-line counting with two or more of each symbol missing. Over 60 test days, our solver needed 21.3 steps on average for Sprout, 35.8 for Bloom and 47.3 for Grove.

Questions and answers

Are binary puzzles and Takuzu the same?

Yes, they are names for the same family. Rule sets vary slightly between publishers, mainly in whether identical rows are allowed.

Can a line have two equal symbols together?

Yes. Two are fine; three are not.

Why are boards always even-sized?

Equal counts need an even number of squares per line.

What if I find two ways to finish a line?

Then that line alone does not decide those squares. Look at crossing columns or another line.

Is guessing ever needed?

Not on our boards. Each one is solved by our solver with the techniques of its level before publication.
