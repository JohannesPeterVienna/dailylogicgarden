Getting started

Logic puzzles for beginners: where to start

Logic puzzles need no math and no trivia – only the willingness to say “because”. This page shows what the easiest board of each of our five puzzles actually asks of you, how the three levels build up, what to do when you get stuck, and how to make the boards comfortable to see and to tap.

What a logic puzzle is

A logic puzzle gives you rules and a few clues. Every step follows from them – no knowledge, no luck. All five puzzles on this site have exactly one solution, so there is always a next certain step.

The step counts are not minutes; they say how many separate deductions our solver wrote down. Flower Beds has the shortest path because one planted flower rules out many squares at once. Sun & Shade has more steps, but they are the same three small moves again and again, and its two rules fit on a sticky note – for most people a good first puzzle. Who Planted What is the one to choose if you prefer reading clues to scanning a grid. You can watch any of these boards being solved one step at a time in the walkthrough.

How the levels build up

Sprout needs one rule at a time. Bloom asks you to combine two facts – a count and a position, a clue and a grid. Grove adds a short look ahead or an argument about several lines at once. The level is set by the hardest technique a board needs, not by its size; the technique reference lists all of them with how often our boards needed each one.

When you get stuck

Being stuck means a fact is hiding in plain sight. Look at the smallest region, the fullest line, the clue you haven’t used. Then press Why?: it shows one step, its reason and the name of the technique, not the answer. Read it, find the same pattern elsewhere on the board, and only then apply it.

Comfortable play: larger squares, keyboard, paper

  • No clock to beat. There is no countdown and no streak. The time appears only in the result after you finish, and it pauses when you switch away. Hints cost nothing.
  • Zoom. On a computer, press Ctrl and + (Cmd and + on a Mac); the board grows with the page and never needs sideways scrolling.
  • Screen size. Garden Path at Sprout has the fewest and therefore the largest squares (25). The 10 × 10 Sun & Shade Grove board has the smallest ones and is easier on a tablet or computer than on a phone.
  • Not by color alone. Beds have patterns, names and thick borders; suns and shades are different shapes. The site follows your device’s light or dark setting.
  • Keyboard. Every puzzle can be played with the keyboard alone: the arrow keys move an outlined cursor and a letter key places the mark. Screen readers announce each square, for example “C4, Iris bed, open”.
  • Touch. A tap is enough – no long presses. Garden Path accepts taps instead of dragging, and Undo takes back a tap that landed wrong.
  • Paper. The printable packs print a week of boards, one per page, in black and white.

We make no medical promises: puzzles are a pastime, and we do not claim any effect on memory or health.

The rules in one line each

  • Sun & Shade: no three alike next to each other; half suns, half shades in every line.
  • Flower Beds: one flower per row, column and bed; flowers never touch.
  • Tents & Trees: each tree gets its own tent beside it; tents never touch; numbers count tents.
  • Garden Path: one line through every square, from stone 1, stones in order.
  • Who Planted What: each gardener has one crop, bed and day; use the clues.

If you remember these five lines, you can play everything on this site. The longer explanations on each puzzle page add controls, examples and the first techniques worth learning.

Why reasoning beats guessing

It is tempting to place a piece and see what happens. On a board with a unique solution, that usually leads to a contradiction many moves later, and then you don’t know which of your moves was wrong. Reasoning step by step is slower at first and much faster overall, because you never have to undo. It is also where the satisfaction lies: the solution is not found, it is proven.

After the first weeks

Once Sprout boards go smoothly, move one puzzle at a time to Bloom. The hint names the new technique the first time you need it; the matching guide – Queens-style rules, Tents strategy, binary rules or logic grids – shows it on a real board. Your garden records the level of every solved puzzle, with taller plants for harder levels, so you can see your own progress without scores or rankings.

One last tip: say a step out loud. If you can finish the sentence “this square must be a flower, because …”, the move is safe. If the sentence trails off, it is a guess in disguise – look again or ask for a hint.

Questions and answers

Do I need to be good at math?

No. You need to count to ten at most. Logic puzzles are about consequences, not calculations.

How long is a Sprout puzzle?

We do not measure players’ times. As a size guide: our solver wrote down between 5 and 34 small steps for the Sprout boards of the first 150 days; the table above gives the average per puzzle.

Are hints cheating?

No. A hint explains one step, and the garden rewards every solved puzzle the same way.

Will I lose my progress if I close the page?

No. Every move is saved in your browser automatically, and no account is needed.

Is there a large-print option?

Use your browser zoom or the printable packs; both give larger boards without losing anything.

Can children play?

The Sprout levels suit older children with an adult. Please note the site shows advertising and is designed for adults.
