How to play Queens-style puzzles: rules in one minute

Queens-style puzzles have only three rules, and you can learn them in a minute. This page explains them with our Flower Beds boards, shows what a single placement rules out, and lists the first moves and the most common mistakes.

The three rules

A Queens-style board is a square grid – often 6 × 6 to 9 × 9 – divided into colored regions. On Daily Logic Garden the regions are flower beds and the pieces are flowers; elsewhere they are crowns, stars or queens. The rules are the same:

  1. Exactly one piece in every row and every column.
  2. Exactly one piece in every region. An 8 × 8 board therefore has eight regions and eight pieces.
  3. No two pieces touch – not side by side and not diagonally.

That is all. There is no rule about long diagonals: despite the name, the pieces do not attack like chess queens across the whole board. They only block the eight squares directly around them. In puzzle books this genre is called a one-star Star Battle; “Queens” is a popular nickname that comes from a daily game on a professional networking site.

What one piece rules out

Every placement removes a lot at once. In the picture, one flower removes its row (because the row is now complete), its column (same reason), every other square of its bed, and the ring of neighbors that also catches squares of two other rows and columns. On a 5 × 5 board that is more than half of the board decided by one move. This is why the puzzle feels slow at the start and fast at the end: the first two or three placements carry all the difficulty.

The flip side: a wrong placement also removes a lot, and you may not notice for several moves. If the board suddenly leaves a bed with no open square, the mistake is usually two or three flowers back. On our boards, the Why? hint checks your marks first and points at a wrong one before giving any new step.

Two or three beds squeezed into the same two or three linesThose lines belong to them; everything else there is emptyGrove

Small beds are the friendliest starting point. A bed of one square is a gift: the piece is there. A bed of two squares side by side means the two squares directly above them (and below them) are touched by either option, so they can be crossed out. A bed shaped like a short bar along an edge often claims its row.

If nothing jumps out, count. Which rows still have many open squares, which have few? A row with only two open squares that both lie in the same bed decides that bed’s piece is in that row – the rest of the bed is empty. That is the most useful single idea in the whole genre, and the next guides build on it.

Common slips

  • Forgetting diagonals. Two pieces at B2 and C3 touch at a corner and break rule 3. Our board outlines such pairs in red.
  • Counting a region twice. A long, winding bed can reach into four or five rows. It still gets only one piece.
  • Guessing early. Every board on this site has exactly one solution reachable by logic. If you feel you must guess, there is a deduction you haven’t seen yet – press Why? instead.
  • Relying on color alone. Similar shades are easy to confuse late at night or on a dim screen. Our beds also have patterns, names and thick borders; use them.

Board sizes and how many arrangements exist

Without the regions, how many ways are there to place one piece per row and column with no two touching? For small boards you can count it by computer, and we did: 2 ways on a 4 × 4 board, 14 on 5 × 5, 90 on 6 × 6, 646 on 7 × 7 and 5,242 on 8 × 8. (These numbers are a known sequence in combinatorics, related to an old problem about kings on a chessboard.) The regions have to whittle those thousands down to one. That is why a well-made board has beds of very different shapes: the irregular ones carry the information.

On Daily Logic Garden, Sprout boards are 6 × 6, Bloom 7 × 7 and Grove 8 × 8, but the level is set by the techniques a board requires. Ready to play? Today’s boards are on the Flower Beds page; when the simple moves run out, continue with Star Battle strategy.

Questions and answers

Do pieces block diagonals like a chess queen?

No. A piece blocks only its row, its column, its region and the eight squares directly around it. Squares further away on a diagonal are not affected.

Is this the same as Star Battle?

It is the one-star version of Star Battle. Two-star Star Battle boards, usually 10 × 10, need two pieces per row, column and region and are considerably harder.

How do I know a board is fair?

A fair board has exactly one solution and can be solved without guessing. Every board on this site is checked for both before it appears.

Which size should I start with?

Start with 6 × 6 (our Sprout level). The rules are identical at every size; larger boards simply need more steps and more advanced techniques.

Why do people say “Queens” if the pieces are not queens?

The nickname comes from a popular daily version that uses crown icons. We describe the genre that way only so people searching for it find the rules.
