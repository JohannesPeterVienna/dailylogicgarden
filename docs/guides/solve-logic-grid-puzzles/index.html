How to solve logic grid puzzles without guessing

Logic grid puzzles give you a story and a few clues, and ask who did what. They look wordy, but a calm, repeatable method solves them every time. Here it is, using our Who Planted What puzzles as examples.

Setting up

A logic grid has categories (gardeners, crops, beds, days) with the same number of items each. Every item of one category goes with exactly one item of each other category. The grid has one small square block per pair of categories; a ● means “these belong together”, a ✕ means “they don’t”.

Before reading clues, notice which categories are ordered. Beds numbered from the gate and days of the week are ordered; names and crops are not. Clues like “earlier” and “next to” only apply to ordered categories.

Step 1: Direct clues

Go through the list once and enter everything a clue states outright: “X did not plant Y” is a ✕; “X planted either A or B” crosses out every other option for X. Comparison clues also hide a direct fact: “Ada planted earlier than the kale grower” means Ada did not grow the kale.

Step 2: One per row, one per column

A ● in a row crosses out the rest of the row and the column in that block. A row with only one open square left gets the ●. This step alone often doubles what you know after the direct clues.

Step 3: Carry facts between blocks

When two items belong together, they share all their relations. Copy the ✕s and ●s of one onto the other. The more general form: if Ada’s crop can only be basil or kale, and neither basil nor kale is in Bed 1, Ada is not in Bed 1.

Step 4: Ordering clues

For “X before Y”, X can’t be last and Y can’t be first. For “Y the day after X”, X can’t be last and Y can’t be first either – and as soon as you narrow down X, Y follows. The strongest move is to list where X can still be and where Y can still be, and remove every position that has no partner. Our hints say it like this: “Clue 3 can only fit if Ines is on Tuesday or Thursday, which rules out Tuesday for the sunflower grower.”

Step 5: Loop

Repeat steps 2 to 4 until nothing changes. If you are stuck, re-read each clue and ask what it says now, given everything you know. Clues that were useless at the start often decide the end. And never guess – every puzzle on our site has a unique solution that the method above reaches.

A worked mini example

Take three gardeners – Ada, Bram, Cleo – three crops – basil, kale, peas – and three beds. Clues: (1) Bram did not plant the kale. (2) The person who planted the peas worked in Bed 3. (3) Cleo worked in a bed closer to the gate than Bram did. (4) Ada worked in Bed 3.

Step 1 enters clue 1 as a ✕, clue 2 as a ● between peas and Bed 3, clue 4 as a ● between Ada and Bed 3, and clue 3 as two ✕s: Cleo is not in Bed 3 (someone is farther from the gate) and Bram is not in Bed 1 (someone is closer). Step 2: Ada is in Bed 3, so Bram and Cleo are not; Bram is then in Bed 2 (not 1, not 3), and Cleo in Bed 1. Step 3: Ada is in Bed 3 and the peas are in Bed 3, so Ada planted the peas. That leaves basil and kale for Bram and Cleo. Back to step 1: clue 1 already crossed out the kale for Bram, so step 2 gives Bram the basil, and the last open square gives Cleo the kale. Step 3 carries these into the Crops × Beds grid: basil in Bed 2, kale in Bed 1. Solution: Cleo, kale, Bed 1; Bram, basil, Bed 2; Ada, peas, Bed 3. Check it against every clue once more – a habit worth keeping. Notice that clue 1 looked useless at the start and decided the end; when a puzzle stops, re-read each clue completely before assuming you missed something.

Typical mistakes

  • Reading “either … or” as “at least one”. In these puzzles it means exactly one, because each person has one item per category.
  • Forgetting that a comparison means two different people.
  • Updating one grid and not the others. After every ●, ask: what does this carry over to the other blocks?

Questions and answers

Do I need to fill every block?

No, only enough to identify every match. The extra blocks help you carry facts.

What is the fastest way to get better?

Write every direct clue first, every time. Most stuck positions come from a clue fact not yet entered.

Should I use notes outside the grid?

For ordering clues, a short line of positions (1 2 3 4) with possible names under each helps a lot.

Are there puzzles with more categories?

Yes, classic magazine puzzles often have five categories of five items. The method is the same.

Why does the famous Zebra Puzzle feel so hard?

It has five categories of five items and many relative clues, so you need many rounds of steps 3 and 4.
