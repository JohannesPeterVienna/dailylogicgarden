Star Battle strategy: 7 techniques from easy to expert

You know the rules; now you want to stop staring at the board. These seven techniques cover everything our solver needs for the one-star boards on this site – in the order it tries them – plus a few habits that make each technique easier to spot.

7Three (or more) regions, three linesThe same counting with bigger groupsGrove

The levels on Daily Logic Garden are defined by this list: a Sprout board can be finished with techniques 1–2, Bloom needs something from 3–5, and Grove needs 6 or 7 at least once. In our test of 60 Grove boards (November 2 to December 31, 2026), every one of them needed a multi-region step.

1–2: Clear the surroundings, find the last square

After every placement, cross off its row, its column, its region and the eight squares around it. Our board does this for you with small dots if you leave “Show blocked squares” switched on – but on paper it is the habit that matters most. Most errors on paper come from a single forgotten diagonal neighbor.

Then scan for a row, column or region with only one open square. Scan regions first: they are usually smaller than lines, so they run out of room sooner. On Sprout boards these two ideas solve everything, which makes Sprout a good place to build the scanning habit.

3–4: Confinement in both directions

Look at a region and ask: in which rows can its piece still be? If the answer is “only row 4”, then row 4’s piece belongs to this region, and every square of row 4 outside the region is empty. The same works with columns.

The mirror image is just as useful. Look at a row and ask: in which regions are its open squares? If they all belong to one region, that region’s piece is in this row, and every other square of the region – in other rows – is empty. Players often find one direction and forget the other; train yourself to ask both questions whenever a region or a line gets small.

5: Touch-all

Pick an open square and imagine a piece on it. It would block its row, its column, its region and its neighbors. Now look at the other regions (and lines): does any of them lose all its open squares? Then the imagined piece is impossible, and the square is empty.

This sounds like trial and error, but it isn’t: you check one square against one region, with no further consequences. In practice it is found visually. A region of two or three squares in an L shape, sitting next to a square that touches all of them, is the classic picture. Our hint phrases it as “A flower on D5 would touch or share a line with every open square of the Rose bed, leaving it no place for its flower, so D5 stays empty.” In our test, 45 of 60 Bloom boards and all 60 Grove boards used it.

6–7: Groups of regions and lines

Technique 3 is the case of one region in one line. Generalize it: if two regions are both confined to the same two rows, those two rows must give their two pieces to these two regions – there is no room for anyone else. Every other square in those rows is empty. With three regions in three rows the logic is identical, just harder to see.

The reverse works too: if all open squares of two columns lie inside two regions, those regions must place their pieces in those columns, and their squares elsewhere are empty. Our dedicated page, the “three beds, three rows” trick, shows a real Grove position and how to spot the pattern.

Habits that make the techniques visible

  • Mark empties aggressively. Techniques 3–7 all depend on knowing where a region can still go. Crosses make that visible.
  • Work from small to large. Small regions and nearly full lines give the most information.
  • Switch viewpoints. Every few moves, look at rows, then columns, then regions. Each view hides patterns the others show.
  • Use the hint as a teacher. When you press Why?, read the technique name in the sentence and look for the same pattern elsewhere before applying it.

Questions and answers

Is touch-all a form of guessing?

No. It checks a single square against a single region without following any chain of consequences. If the region would be left without options, the square is empty – that is a direct deduction.

Do these techniques work for two-star Star Battle?

The ideas carry over, but the counting changes: with two stars per region, two regions confined to two rows need four stars from them. The pattern is the same, the numbers double.

Why does the solver try techniques in a fixed order?

So that hints always show the simplest available step, and so that a board’s level reflects the hardest technique it truly needs.

Which technique is most often missed?

A likely candidate is the reverse direction of confinement – a row whose open squares all lie in one region – because it asks you to look at a line instead of a shape. If you are stuck, check that direction explicitly.

How many steps does a Grove board take?

Our solver records 20 logical steps on average for Grove Flower Beds boards, compared with 6 for Sprout.
