Tents and Trees rules and solving strategy

Tents and Trees looks like a camping sketch and plays like a tidy counting puzzle. Here are the rules in full – including the pairing rule many people miss – and the strategy our solver uses, from the first sweep to the look-ahead needed on Grove boards.

The rules

  1. Every tree has exactly one tent of its own, on a square directly above, below, left or right of it.
  2. Tents and trees are paired one to one, so the number of tents equals the number of trees.
  3. No two tents touch, not even diagonally.
  4. The number next to each row and below each column is the exact number of tents in that line.

The opening sweep

Start with three quick passes that need no thought:

  • No tree, no tent. A square with no tree directly beside it can never hold a tent. Mark all of them as grass. This often removes a third of the board.
  • Zero lines. Rows and columns marked 0 are all grass.
  • Exact fits. If a line needs as many tents as it has open squares, all of them are tents.

Then look at trees with a single open side and no tent next to them yet: that side is the tent. After every new tent, mark its eight neighbors as grass and re-check the lines it touched. These moves are the whole Sprout level.

Fitting tents into a line

Tents in the same row can’t be neighbors. So a run of open squares can hold at most half its length, rounded up: one tent in a run of 1 or 2, two in a run of 3 or 4. If a row needs two tents and its only open squares are B, C and D, the tents must be on B and D – C is grass.

Add the runs of a line together: if the maxima add up exactly to the number of tents needed, every odd-length run is forced into its alternating pattern. Our solver calls this “line fit” and it is the most common Bloom technique – 51 of 60 Bloom boards in our test period used it.

Using the pairing rule

Pairing turns into deductions in two ways. A tent with only one tree beside it belongs to that tree. Once a tree has its own tent, any square whose only neighboring trees are already paired can’t be a tent – it would have no tree to belong to. And a tree whose neighboring tents all belong to other trees still needs its own: if it has one open side left, that is its tent.

Keep track of pairs with a small mental arrow from tree to tent. Our hints spell out the pairs they rely on, e.g. “Every tree next to E3 already has its own tent (D3 → D2, E4 → E5)”.

The one-tent look-ahead

When nothing else works, take one open square and imagine a tent on it. Mark its eight neighbors as grass – only those – and check two things: does any row or column now have too few open squares for its number? Does any tree lose its last free side? If yes, the square is grass.

This is the Grove technique, and every Grove board in our test needed it at least once. It is bounded: one tent, one ring of grass, one check. If you find yourself imagining a second tent on top of the first, stop – the boards on this site never require that.

Common mistakes

  • Placing a tent diagonally next to its tree. Diagonal does not pair.
  • Letting two trees share one tent. Count trees and tents; the numbers must match.
  • Forgetting that a tent blocks squares in the neighboring rows too, which changes their counts.

How our levels map to these steps

Sprout boards (6 × 6) are solved by the opening sweep and lone trees. Bloom boards (7 × 7) also need line fitting or pairing. Grove boards (8 × 8) need the one-tent look-ahead at least once. Over the 60 test days, Grove boards had 13.3 trees on average and took our solver 17.1 logical steps; Sprout boards had 7.5 trees and took 8.9 steps. If a Grove board feels stuck, it is almost always a look-ahead moment: pick a square next to a tree that has few options and test it.

Questions and answers

Can a tent stand next to two trees?

Yes, but it belongs to only one of them. The other tree needs a different tent.

Can a tree be next to another tree?

Yes. Trees can stand side by side; only tents have the no-touching rule.

What does a struck-through number mean on the board?

That row or column already has all its tents. Every open square left in it is grass.

Is the look-ahead trial and error?

It is a single bounded check – one imagined tent and its immediate neighbors – not a chain of guesses. The boards never need deeper searches.

Where does the puzzle come from?

Tents puzzles have appeared in puzzle magazines and collections for decades. Our boards are generated by our own code; nothing is copied.
