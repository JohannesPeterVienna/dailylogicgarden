The “three beds, three rows” trick explained

When several beds can only use the same few rows, those rows are spoken for – and everything else in them is empty. This one counting idea unlocks most hard Flower Beds and Star Battle boards. Here it is, with a real position from our Grove level.

Advertisement

The idea in plain words

Every row gets exactly one flower, and every bed gets exactly one flower. Suppose you find three beds whose remaining open squares all lie in rows 5, 6 and 7. Those three beds need three flowers, and those flowers can only be in rows 5, 6 and 7. But those three rows only have three flowers to give. So rows 5, 6 and 7 are completely used up by these three beds – no other bed can place a flower there. Every open square in rows 5–7 that belongs to some other bed is empty.

It is pure counting: three beds need three flowers; three rows offer three flowers; they must match exactly.

A real Grove position

This is what our hint said at that moment: “The Sage, Thyme and Poppy beds fit entirely inside rows 5, 6 and 7, so those three lines are reserved for them and every other open square there stays empty.”

Notice that none of the three beds is confined to a single row. Each one alone could still go in two or three rows, so the simple “bed inside a line” idea gives nothing. Only the group of three shows the squeeze.

The four versions of the trick

Group confinement in all directions
You noticeYou conclude
k beds whose open squares lie within k rowsOther squares in those rows are empty
k beds whose open squares lie within k columnsOther squares in those columns are empty
k rows whose open squares lie within k bedsOther squares of those beds are empty
k columns whose open squares lie within k bedsOther squares of those beds are empty

With k = 1 this is the familiar “bed in one row” move. With k = 2 and k = 3 it becomes the Grove technique. Larger groups exist, but they are rarely the first thing you find: if k beds fill k rows of an 8 × 8 board with k = 5, then the other three beds fill the other three rows, and the smaller group is usually easier to see.

One check keeps you safe. Count only beds and rows that still need their flower. A bed that already has its flower is finished and does not take part.

How to spot it

  1. Shade with your eyes. For each bed, note the band of rows it still covers – for example “Iris: 2–3”, “Fern: 2–4”, “Mint: 3–4”. On paper, write these bands beside the board.
  2. Look for nested bands. Iris (2–3), Fern (2–4) and Mint (3–4) all fit inside 2–4: three beds, three rows.
  3. Check the rest of those rows. If any other bed still has open squares in rows 2–4, those squares are now empty.
  4. Repeat with columns. The same beds may form a different group in the column direction.

Beds that run along an edge are the usual suspects, because the board edge already limits them.

Why it is not guessing

Nothing is assumed. The argument never says “suppose the Iris flower is in row 2”; it only counts how many flowers a set of rows has to give and how many a set of beds must receive. That is why our solver treats it as a regular logical step, ranks it as the hardest technique on Flower Beds, and lets a Grove board appear only when this step is genuinely needed.

Try it on today’s Grove board on the Flower Beds page. When you press Why? at the right moment, the hint names the beds and the rows. For the full set of techniques, see Star Battle strategy.

A short practice routine

On the next Grove board you play, stop after the first easy moves and write the row band of every unfinished bed in the margin, then the column band. Look for two beds with the same two-row band first; pairs are far more common than triples. Only then look for triples. Once you have found one group on your own, press Why? to compare: if the hint names a different group, both are valid – a board can contain several – and the hint simply reports the first one in its search order. After two or three boards the bands become something you see without writing them down.

Questions and answers

Does the trick work with columns too?

Yes. Replace rows with columns everywhere; the counting is identical.

What if four beds fit in three rows?

Then the board has no solution – four beds cannot share three flowers. On a correct board this means an earlier mark is wrong.

Is this the same as the X-wing in Sudoku?

It is a cousin: both count how many candidates a group of units can take. The puzzles and rules differ, so the patterns look different.

How often is it needed?

On our Grove level, every board needs a group of two or more beds at least once; that is what defines the level.

Does it help in two-star Star Battle?

Yes, with doubled numbers: k regions confined to k rows need 2k stars, which is exactly what those rows offer.
