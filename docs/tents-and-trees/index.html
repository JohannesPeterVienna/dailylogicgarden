Tents and Trees puzzle – play online free

Every tree wants one tent right beside it. Tents may not touch each other, not even at the corners, and the numbers along the edges tell you how many tents stand in each row and column. Three fresh campsites open every day.

How to play Tents & Trees

The board shows trees and two rows of numbers: one number at the end of every row and one under every column. Your job is to pitch tents so that:

  1. Every tree has its own tent on a square directly above, below, left or right of it – not diagonally. Tents and trees pair up one to one, so there are exactly as many tents as trees.
  2. Tents never touch, not side by side and not at a corner.
  3. The numbers are exact: a row marked 2 holds exactly two tents.

Tap an empty square once to lay grass (a square that certainly has no tent), twice for a tent and a third time to clear it. Drag across squares to lay grass on all of them. Keyboard players move with the arrows, press T for a tent, G for grass and Backspace to clear. When a row or column has all its tents, its number fades and is struck through – a quiet signal that the rest of that line is grass.

The pairing rule is what makes this puzzle more than counting. A tent standing between two trees can only belong to one of them, so the other tree still needs a tent of its own somewhere else. Keep that in mind and many “impossible” positions open up.

The look-ahead is still short and concrete: you imagine one tent, cross off the eight squares around it and check a single row, column or tree. It never requires trying out long chains of guesses.

A solved example

How would you start a board like this? First, every square that has no tree directly beside it is grass – it could never hold a tent. On a typical board that removes a third of the squares at once. Next, look at rows and columns marked 0 (all grass) and at lines whose number equals the open squares left (all tents). Then look at trees with only one free side: the tent must go there.

After those first sweeps, count. A row of five that needs two tents and has open squares only at B, C and D can only hold them at B and D, because tents in a line cannot be neighbors. That is the kind of small counting argument the Bloom level is built on; the Tents and Trees strategy guide walks through it with more examples.

The pairing rule, explained

Beginners often forget that tents and trees form couples. A board can satisfy every number and keep every tent apart and still be wrong, because two trees share one tent while a third tent has no tree at all. Our checker uses the full rule: a solution is only accepted if every tent can be matched to a different neighboring tree.

The rule also gives useful deductions. Suppose a tree has exactly one free side left and none of its neighboring tents is free – each of those tents is already the only tent of some other tree. Then the free side must be this tree’s tent. In the other direction, a square whose neighboring trees all have their tents already settled can never hold a tent; it is grass. The “Why?” hint names the pairs it relies on, for example “Every tree next to E3 already has its own tent (D3 → D2, E4 → E5), so a tent on E3 would have no tree.”

Small habits that help

  • Lay grass generously. Grass is never a risk when it follows from a rule, and a board full of grass makes the remaining choices obvious.
  • Work line by line when you are stuck. For each row, ask: how many tents are still missing, and in how many separate gaps could they go? A gap of three open squares can take at most two tents, a gap of one or two squares at most one.
  • Watch the corners of tents. A new tent removes up to eight squares, often from two different rows and columns at once. Re-check those lines immediately.
  • Use the dots. With “Show blocked squares” on, the board marks every square around your tents. You can switch this off if you prefer to do it by hand.

When you finish, the page plants a small fir in your garden and gives you a result line you can share – the level, the date, your time and the number of hints, without revealing a single tent.

Questions and answers

Can a tent touch its own tree diagonally?

No. A tent must be directly above, below, left or right of its tree. Diagonal neighbors do not count as a pair, although a tent may of course stand diagonally next to some other tree.

Can two tents be next to the same tree?

Yes, as long as each tent can be paired with a different tree. A tree may have several tents around it; only one of them is its own.

Are the puzzles the same for everyone?

Yes. The board is created in your browser from the date and the level, so every player gets the same campsite on the same day without any server or account.

How big are the boards?

Sprout boards are 6 × 6, Bloom boards 7 × 7 and Grove boards 8 × 8. The level is decided by the reasoning a board needs, so a Grove board is not just a larger Sprout.

Can I print the puzzle?

Yes. The Print button prints today’s board with its numbers and trees in black and white, and the printable packs collect a whole week on separate pages.
