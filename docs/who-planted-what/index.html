Who Planted What: beginner logic grid puzzles

Four gardeners, four crops, four beds – and a handful of clues. Mark what cannot be true, spot what must be, and work out who planted what, where and when. A small, friendly logic grid every day.

How to play

Each gardener planted exactly one crop in exactly one bed (on Bloom and Grove days, also on exactly one day of the week). No two gardeners share a crop, a bed or a day. Bed 1 is the one nearest the garden gate, so “closer to the gate” means a lower bed number.

Below the clues you see small grids, one for every pair of categories: Gardeners × Crops, Gardeners × Beds, Crops × Beds and so on. Tap a square once for ✕ (“these two don’t go together”), twice for ● (“these two belong together”) and a third time to clear it. With “Fill ✕ automatically” switched on, a ● crosses out the rest of its row and column in that grid for you. Keyboard players move with the arrow keys inside a grid, jump to the next grid with Tab, and press Y for yes, N for no.

The puzzle is solved when every gardener is matched with a crop, a bed and – on four-category days – a day. The Crops × Beds style grids are there to help you think; filling them completely is optional.

The method in three moves

  1. Write down the direct clues. “Cleo did not plant the kale” is one ✕. “Dev planted either the peas or the radishes” crosses out every other crop for Dev. A comparison such as “Ada worked in a bed closer to the gate than the kale grower” tells you, among other things, that Ada did not grow the kale.
  2. One per row, one per column. As soon as a row has a ●, the rest of that row is ✕. As soon as a row has only one open square left, that square is the ●.
  3. Carry facts between grids. If two items belong together, they share all their other relations: whatever is crossed out for the basil is crossed out for Ada, and the other way round.

These three moves solve every Sprout puzzle. Bloom adds ordering clues (“planted the day after”) and “it can only be one of these two” reasoning; Grove adds clues that only fit one way once you look at all positions together. The full method, with examples, is in How to solve logic grid puzzles without guessing.

The arrangement counts are simple arithmetic: with four gardeners there are 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24 ways to hand out the crops, 24 ways to hand out the beds and 24 for the days, so 24 × 24 = 576 arrangements with three categories and 24 × 24 × 24 = 13,824 with four. The clues must cut that down to exactly one; our generator checks every one of those arrangements against the clues before a puzzle is published.

Grove puzzles have fewer clues than Bloom puzzles on average, because they contain no direct “X planted Y” statements at all. Each clue says less, so you have to combine more of them.

How the clues are written

Every clue is a plain, true statement about the solution, built from a small set of patterns so that there is no wordplay to decode:

  • “X planted the Y” and “X did not work in Bed 3” – direct facts.
  • “X planted either the basil or the kale” – exactly one of the two is true.
  • “X worked in a bed closer to the gate than Y did” and “X planted earlier in the week than Y did” – an order, and X and Y are different people.
  • “X and Y worked in neighboring beds” or “planted on consecutive days” – they are one step apart, in either direction.
  • “Y planted the day after X did” and “Y worked in the bed right after X’s” – exactly one step, in a known direction.

When a hint refers to a clue, that clue is highlighted in the list. Hints always name the clue or the grid fact they use, so you can follow the argument rather than copy a result.

Playing on paper

Logic grids are a classic pencil-and-paper format, and they still work well that way. The Print button prints the clues together with a simple answer table – gardener, crop, bed, day – which many people find easier on paper than the full set of grids. For a whole week at once, use the printable packs. A fresh puzzle appears every day at midnight UTC, and solving it plants a tulip in your garden.

Questions and answers

Do I have to fill every grid?

No. The puzzle counts as solved when every gardener is matched correctly. The grids between the other categories are tools for your reasoning.

What does &ldquo;closer to the gate&rdquo; mean?

Bed 1 is nearest the gate and Bed 4 is farthest away, so “closer to the gate” means a lower bed number.

Does &ldquo;either … or&rdquo; allow both?

No. A gardener plants exactly one crop, so “either the basil or the kale” means exactly one of the two.

Are the gardeners real people?

No. The first names are picked at random from a fixed list for each puzzle; they do not refer to anyone.

Is there always one answer?

Yes. Before a puzzle is published, every possible arrangement is checked against the clues, and exactly one must fit. Our solver must also reach it without guessing.
